Two ways of writing the same model

I’ve been reading Ken Bollen’s new edition of Elements of Structural Equation Models and was stopped short by a passage in §2.3.3, which looks almost too obvious to need saying: a path diagram and a set of structural equations are equivalent. Any structural equation model (SEM) can be written either way, and you can go from one to the other and back again without losing anything.

Every single-headed arrow in the diagram is a coefficient in some equation. Every equation is the set of arrows pointing into one variable. A curved, double-headed arrow is a covariance, usually between two error terms that the equations would otherwise leave as a footnote. Draw the picture and you have written the equations; write the equations and you could draw the picture.

So far, so textbook. What caught me was the question underneath. If the two forms say exactly the same thing, why do we need both? And why do I think differently depending on which one is in front of me?

With the diagram, I see structure at a glance: what’s connected to what, which variables are observed (boxes) and which are latent (circles), where measurement error comes in. With the equations, I see what gets estimated, what’s constrained, and whether the whole thing is even identified. Then there’s a third form, the code I type into sem or lavaan, and a fourth, the output that comes back. The model lives in all of these at once, and a good part of the thinking seems to happen in the translation from one to another.

That’s the thread I want to pull on here. A good notation makes these translations easy, suggestive and (ideally) reversible. A weak or missing notation shows up as a translation you can’t make, or can make only in one direction.

A single symbol

This isn’t a new thought for me. It goes back to my first serious programming language, APL.

APL had a symbol, ⌹ (“quad-divide”, or “domino”), for matrix inversion. Given a square matrix A, ⌹A was its inverse. So far, just a compact way to write \(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\). But used between two arguments, Y ⌹ X meant “divide Y by the matrix X”, and when X had more rows than columns, it gave the least squares solution. The whole of multiple regression, \(\mathbf{b} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}\), was

B ← Y ⌹ X

That one line did something the textbook formula didn’t. It made regression feel like division: finding the \(\mathbf{b}\) that best solves \(\mathbf{y} \approx \mathbf{X} \mathbf{b}\), just as \(y / x\) solves \(y = x b\) for numbers. The formula with its transposes and inverse tells you how to compute the answer. The APL version told me what the answer was.

I’ve carried that idea around ever since, through matrix algebra, model formulas in R, data ellipses and, now, SEM diagrams. It turns out someone had given it a name long before I thought about it.

Iverson: Notation as a tool of thought

APL was invented by Kenneth E. Iverson, a Canadian from Alberta who did his doctorate at Harvard, taught there, and then spent the 1960s and ’70s at IBM developing the language. He later moved to I. P. Sharp Associates in Toronto, a company built largely on APL time-sharing, just down the road from me.

In 1979 Iverson received the ACM Turing Award “for his pioneering effort in programming languages and mathematical notation resulting in what the computing field now knows as APL.” His award lecture, delivered on October 29, 1979, and published the following year (Iverson 1980), was titled Notation as a Tool of Thought. I’ve borrowed his title for this post, with gratitude.

A detail that’s easy to miss: APL did not start as a programming language. It began as a notation, a way for Iverson to write about algorithms clearly when teaching, and was used on paper for several years before anyone implemented it on a computer. The notation came first; being able to run it came later.

Iverson opens with the claim that notation matters to thinking, not just to writing thoughts down. He cites the systems of nomenclature of Lavoisier in chemistry and Linnaeus in botany, and then turns to mathematicians, by way of Florian Cajori’s A History of Mathematical Notations. Two quotations set the tone:

By relieving the brain of all unnecessary work, a good notation sets it free to concentrate on more advanced problems, and in effect increases the mental power of the race.

— A. N. Whitehead

The quantity of meaning compressed into small space by algebraic signs, is another circumstance that facilitates the reasonings we are accustomed to carry on by their aid.

— Charles Babbage

“Increases the mental power of the race” is a big claim, even for Whitehead. But anyone who has tried to do long division in Roman numerals knows what he meant.

Iverson’s argument is that mathematical notation, for all its power, has two serious weaknesses. It isn’t universal: it has to be read differently by topic, by author, even from one page to the next. And it can’t be executed. Programming languages have the opposite profile: universal and unambiguous, and you can run them, so you can experiment with an idea. But they are, he says, “decidedly inferior to mathematical notation” as tools of thought. His thesis is that you can have both in one language.

What makes a notation good?

Iverson lists five characteristics of a good notation:

  • ease of expressing constructs arising in problems
  • suggestivity
  • ability to subordinate detail
  • economy
  • amenability to formal proofs

His examples are small and charming. In APL, ⍳5 is the vector 1 2 3 4 5, and +/ means “sum over”, so +/⍳5 is 15. Stack up rows of 1, 2, …, 5 atoms and you have a triangle; +/⍳5 counts them. Put a second, upside-down triangle beside the first and every row now has 6 atoms, 5 rows of them, so twice the sum is \(5 \times 6\). The notation all but writes the general result for you:

+/⍳N  ↔  ((N+1)×N)÷2

This is the sum that the young Gauss supposedly found in his head in primary school. But in Iverson’s hands the point is less the answer than the way the notation led to it. Once you’ve written +/⍳5, it’s natural to ask about ×/⍳5 (the factorial, 120), or +\⍳5, the running sums 1 3 6 10 15 (the triangular numbers), and on to the tetrahedral ones. That’s suggestivity: the form of one expression suggests others worth trying.

I won’t use Iverson’s list as a formal scorecard for everything that follows. It works better as a set of questions to keep in the back of your mind. When I look at a path diagram, a model formula, or a ggplot() call, what does it make easy to say? What does it suggest? What detail does it let me ignore, and what does it hide that I shouldn’t have ignored?
