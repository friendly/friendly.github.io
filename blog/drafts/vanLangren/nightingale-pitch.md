Subject: Pitch: The 393-year-old cipher hidden inside the first known statistical graph

Hi Nightingale editors,

I'd like to pitch an article about a strange loose end in the history of data visualization that just got tied off — after nearly 400 years, and after I'd already gotten it wrong once in print.

**Short version:** In 1644, Michael Florent van Langren published what my co-authors and I identified in a 2010 *American Statistician* paper as the first known statistical graph — a one-dimensional dot plot showing twelve astronomers' wildly disagreeing estimates of the longitude distance from Toledo to Rome. Van Langren built the graph to make a visual argument to the King of Spain: existing longitude methods were hopelessly imprecise, and he alone had a fix. He never said what the fix was — he printed it in cipher on the same page, promising to reveal the plaintext for a suitable reward. We reproduced the ciphertext in our supplementary materials, speculated a little, and moved on.

In 2020/2021, an amateur codebreaker actually solved it. The plaintext isn't the lunar-crater method everyone (including me) assumed van Langren was hiding — it describes a water clock. Which means the interpretation I put into a peer-reviewed paper about *why* van Langren chose a graph over a table was built on a guess about his method that the cipher itself proves wrong.

**Why this fits Nightingale:** it's a story about a graph that is genuinely a landmark in the field — arguably the first purpose-built statistical chart — but it's also a story about how the history we write about data visualization is provisional, not settled. New evidence (a cracked cipher, in this case) can undo a source's own account of one of its foundational objects. I think that's a useful thing for this community to sit with.

**Status:** the piece is drafted and complete, currently living as a post on my blog for reference: https://friendly.github.io/blog/posts/2026-07-van-langren/. It runs long for a digital piece as written (~4,800 words) — happy to trim it to fit your digital range, or if it's a better fit for a longer treatment, I'd welcome guidance on where to cut. It includes the original 1628 letter and 1644 graph images, a reconstruction of van Langren's dot plot in ggplot2 with the reasoning behind the design choices, the cipher image itself, and a walkthrough of the structural cryptanalysis (interleaved streams + digit substitution) that cracked it.

I'm a member of the Data Visualization Society and happy to convert this into a Google Doc with an accompanying image folder per your submission guidelines as soon as there's interest — just didn't want to do the reformatting work until I knew it was a fit.

A little about me: I'm Michael Friendly, a professor and historian of data visualization; I co-authored the 2010 paper that first identified van Langren's graph as a landmark in the field, so this piece closes a loop I opened fifteen years ago.
