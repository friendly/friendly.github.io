library(dplyr)
library(ggplot2)
library(ggrepel)

data(peng, package = "heplots")    # palmerpenguins, 333 complete cases

col <- c(Adelie = "#F37A00", Chinstrap = "#6A3D9A", Gentoo = "#33a02c")

# the named birds, by row number in heplots::peng
named <- tibble::tribble(
  ~id, ~name,         ~namer,
  283, "Cyrano",       "Thompson",
  124, "The BFG",      "Thompson",
  304, "Tinkerbell",   "Thompson",
  166, "Average Joe",  "Thompson",
   10, "Socrates?",    "Friendly",
   35, "Falstaff?",    "Friendly"
)

peng <- peng |>
  mutate(id         = row_number(),
         bill_shape = log(bill_length) - log(bill_depth),
         stoutness  = log(body_mass) / 3 - log(flipper_length)) |>
  group_by(species) |>
  mutate(z_shape = c(scale(bill_shape)),
         z_stout = c(scale(stoutness))) |>
  ungroup() |>
  left_join(named, by = "id") |>
  mutate(label = ifelse(is.na(name), NA, paste0(name, " (", id, ")")))
birds <- filter(peng, !is.na(name))

Overheard at Biscoe Point

“Hey Bro, have you noticed? All these scientists measured us up the wazoo, published papers, and now our data is famous, used to teach statistics and data graphics all over the world. And they don’t even know our names, even with those tags on our flippers.”

“And why didn’t they bother to give us the Big Five personality scales? Those orange Adelies are often narcissistic extroverts, not very open to experience.”

They have a point. The Palmer penguins are identified only by numbers: their row numbers in a data frame. Even those aren’t stable. They depend on whether the few birds with missing measurements were dropped: Cyrano, whom you’ll meet shortly, is number 294 in palmerpenguins::penguins and number 283 in heplots::peng. This post is an attempt to right this injustice to the 344 birds on Biscoe, Dream and Torgersen islands near Palmer Station who allowed themselves to be measured for science from 2007 to 2009.

A few of them do have names. Cara Thompson named four: Cyrano, the BFG, Tinkerbell and Average Joe. I borrowed Cyrano for my book, and added a Hook Nose of my own. So the questions for this post are: what makes each of these birds unusual, and do their names fit?

Naming names

I first got to know the Palmer penguins when I found that they made good examples for methods of multivariate data visualization. I adopted them as a running example in my book, Visualizing Multivariate Data and Models in R, where they turn up in nearly every chapter.

Three noteworthy birds

In Chapter 4, I went looking for multivariate outliers. As in the Bob Marley song, I found “three little birds, at my doorstep.” As I hope to say to my Palmer friends, “Don’t worry about a thing, ’cause every little thing gonna be all right.”

The main tool is the squared Mahalanobis distance, \(D^2\), of each bird from the centroid of all four measures (bill length and depth, flipper length, body mass), taking their variances and correlations into account. Three birds came out on top: cases 283, 10 and 35. A chi-square QQ plot showed that only one of them, 283, is a true outlier. The other two are just the most unusual of an ordinary lot.

Code 
library(heplots)
vars <- c("bill_length", "bill_depth", "flipper_length", "body_mass")
DSQ <- Mahalanobis(peng[, vars])

cqplot(peng[, vars],
       id.n = 3,
       col = col[peng$species],
       pch = c(19, 17, 15)[peng$species],
       ref.col = "grey",
       what = "Penguin numeric variables",
       cex.lab = 1.25)

Plotting bill length against bill depth with those three labeled, two of them looked odd for their species. Case 283, a Chinstrap, has a remarkably long bill, so I called her “Cyrano”, after the long-nosed hero of Edmond Rostand’s play. Case 10, an Adelie with a short, deep bill, became “Hook Nose”. Case 35 looked unremarkable in that view and stayed nameless.

Code 
three <- order(DSQ, decreasing = TRUE)[1:3]
peng_plot <- peng |> mutate(note = id %in% three)

ggplot(peng_plot,

The names stuck, at least for me. Cyrano reappears throughout the book: dancing around the edges of a grand tour, at the extreme ends of a biplot, as the most influential case in a multivariate linear model, and finally getting a weight of zero in a robust fit. “Bye-bye Cyrano—it’s been fun—but we can get along nicely without you.” That is the point of a name: a reader can follow Cyrano from chapter to chapter in a way they can’t follow “case 283”.

Where “Cyrano” came from

I thought I had made up the name. It turns out I hadn’t. I’d seen it in Cara Thompson’s poster for useR! 2022, Level Up Your Labels: Tips and Tricks for Annotating Plots, filed it away, and forgotten where it came from. My code for the book still had a comment, # From Cara Thompson, above her lines that picked out the birds (with her name misspelled, which didn’t help).

Her poster is a lesson in annotation. Its plot, titled “Perfectly proportional penguins—with a few exceptions!”, shows flipper length against bill length, and replaces a legend and a lot of explanation with a few well-placed text boxes. Four birds get names and a sentence each:

  • The BFG: “Sitting comfortably within the cluster of Gentoos, this Adelie penguin defies the predictions of his species on both flipper length and bill length.”
  • Tinkerbell: “The smallest penguin in the set defies the odds on weight for her species and flipper and bill length.”
  • Average Joes: “The Gentoos tend to stick to their trend, with very few outliers. Heavier penguins have longer flippers and longer bills.”
  • Cyrano: “With an exceptionally long bill and the shortest flippers bar one among the Chinstraps, this little guy is sure to stand out!”

She picked these birds by eye, from one plot. As we’ll see, every one of her descriptions holds up, except perhaps “little guy”: Cyrano is a female.

Who gets a label?

Thompson’s poster had a second effect on me. In her plot, and in mine in the book, the labeled birds were chosen by hand, and the labels were added by a small piece of ggplot2 plumbing: compute something, make a logical variable for the cases to label, then add a geom_text() layer for just those rows. It works, but you have to do it again for every plot.

Base R graphics handle this better than ggplot2, thanks to the car package. Nearly every plotting function in car takes an argument like id = list(n = 3, method = "mahal"), and labels the 3 points furthest from the centroid, with no further work. The method can be "x", "y", "r" for residuals, or a vector such as Cook’s distances. Labeling unusual points becomes a decision about which criterion, not a chore.

I pulled that selection logic out into a function, heplots::noteworthy(), which takes x, y, n and a method, and returns the row numbers of the points to label. The criterion needn’t come from the two variables on the axes. Here it finds the book’s three birds, using \(D^2\) on all four measures, for a plot of just two:

Code 
noteworthy(peng$bill_length, peng$flipper_length, n = 3, method = DSQ)
#> [1] 283  10  35

That isn’t what Thompson would have found with a formula. The default, method = "mahal", on the two variables in her plot picks out a different set:

Code 
noteworthy(peng$bill_length, peng$flipper_length, n = 6, method = "mahal")
#> [1] 283 179 314 272 329 297

Cyrano (283) is first again, but the rest (179, 314, 272, …) are birds on the edges of the overall cloud, which spans three species. None of the BFG, Tinkerbell or Average Joe is on that list. Her eye was doing something the formula wasn’t: judging each bird against its own species. I’ll come back to that.

The next step was to make this a ggplot2 layer, stat_noteworthy(), so that any plot could say which points to label in the same vocabulary. With help from the ggplot2 extenders club, I got a working version. Getting it generally right turned out to be harder:

  • With groups, should it pick the \(n\) most unusual points overall, or \(n\) in each group? The usual ggplot2 habit is per group. A statistician usually wants the most unusual points in the plot as a whole. Both are reasonable, so it should probably be an option.
  • With facets, the selection should be done separately in each panel.
  • Higher dimensions: the penguins’ case is the hardest. The criterion (\(D^2\) over four variables) lives in more dimensions than the plot shows. A ggplot2 stat sees only the variables mapped to aesthetics, so there’s no clean way to hand it the others.

So stat_noteworthy() is still in the development folder of heplots, a branch partly leafed out, waiting for another season. Meanwhile the penguins wait for their names.

What makes a penguin unusual?

Code 
birds |>
  select(name, id, species, sex, bill_length, bill_depth, flipper_length, body_mass,
         z_shape, z_stout) |>
  arrange(desc(abs(z_shape) + abs(z_stout))) |>
  knitr::kable(digits = 2,
               col.names = c("Name", "Case", "Species", "Sex", "Bill length", "Bill depth",
                             "Flipper", "Mass", "z shape", "z stoutness"))
Code 
lim <- c(-4.2, 4.2)
ggplot(peng, aes(z_shape, z_stout, colour = species)) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0, colour = "grey70") +
  geom_vline(xintercept = 0, colour = "grey70") +
  stat_ellipse(aes(group = 1), level = 0.95, colour = "grey40",
               linetype = "dashed", linewidth = 0.7) +
  stat_ellipse(aes(group = 1), level = 0.68, colour = "grey40",
               linewidth = 0.7) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.5, size = 2) +
  geom_point(data = birds, shape = 21, size = 3.5, colour = "black",
             aes(fill = species), stroke = 1.1, show.legend = FALSE) +
  geom_label_repel(data = birds, aes(label = label),
                   size = 4, fontface = "bold", fill = alpha("white", 0.85),
                   box.padding = 0.7, min.segment.length = 0, seed = 2022,
                   show.legend = FALSE) +
  scale_colour_manual(values = col) +
  scale_fill_manual(values = col) +
  coord_equal(xlim = lim, ylim = lim) +
  labs(x = "← stubby    Bill shape, log(length / depth)    slender →",
       y = "← lean    Stoutness, log(mass^(1/3) / flipper)    stout →",
       colour = "Species") +
  guides(colour = guide_legend(override.aes = list(size = 3, alpha = 1))) +
  theme_bw(base_size = 14) +
  theme(legend.position = "inside", legend.position.inside = c(0.88, 0.14),
        legend.background = element_rect(colour = "grey80"))
